## 9.1 How to run a case (in the room)

1. **Listen and take notes.** Write the objective verbatim.
2. **Restate the problem** in one sentence and confirm you have it right.
3. **Ask 2–4 clarifying questions** — scope, geography, timeframe, decision criteria, definition of success. Do not ask more than four.
4. **State your structure** before computing anything ("I'll look at market size, our right to win, the economics, and the risks").
5. **Work the numbers out loud**, with units. Announce results in business terms.
6. **Land a recommendation** with a priority, a rationale and one condition ("subject to access data, proceed").
7. **Name risks and the missing information** without prompting.

**Time budget for a 25-minute case:** 3 min clarifying + structure, 12 min analysis, 5 min findings and recommendation, 5 min questions and risks.

---

## 9.2 The 7-part answer structure (memorise it)

| # | Element | Sentence starter |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Objective** | "The decision is whether to…" |
| 2 | **Framework** | "I'll break this into four parts: …" |
| 3 | **Analysis** | "Using the data provided…" (units + calculation) |
| 4 | **Key findings** | "Three findings stand out: one, …" |
| 5 | **Recommendation** | "I recommend… because…" |
| 6 | **Risks** | "The main risks are…" |
| 7 | **Next steps** | "To de-risk this, the next step would be…" |

**Weak vs strong openers**

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "So basically we should enter because the market is big." | "The decision is whether to launch in India with a specialty product; I'll test market size, our right to win, the launch economics, and the key risks before recommending." |
| "I'll do a SWOT analysis." | "I'll look at size, growth, accessibility, competitive intensity, and our capability fit — then quantify the launch economics." |

---

## 9.3 Defending assumptions

**Rules**
1. **State every assumption** with a number ("I'll assume 10% gross-to-net on a ₹1.2 lakh list price").
2. **Signpost sensitivity**: "If access is worse than assumed, revenue falls to…"
3. **Tie assumptions to data** whenever data exists; label the rest as estimates.
4. **Answer challenges with a test, not a defence**: "That's a fair challenge — I'd validate it with a payer survey before committing spend."

**Common follow-ups and how to handle them**

| Follow-up | Model response |
|---|---|
| "Where did that number come from?" | Name the source (data provided / market benchmark / explicit assumption) and its impact if wrong. |
| "What if the competitor cuts price 20%?" | Quantify the revenue at risk, then discuss responses (value story, contracts, patient support) rather than defending the original number. |
| "Your forecast looks too optimistic." | Offer the bear case, state the trigger conditions, and propose a staged investment. |
| "What data would change your mind?" | Name the single most decision-relevant dataset (access/coverage, persistence, or payer feedback). |
| "Why not just cut the price?" | Run the break-even volume maths (10% price cut needs >11.1% volume growth) and mention margin and GTN effects. |

## Case 1 — Pharmaceutical market entry (fictional: Vantrela Biotech / Dermacalm)

**Problem statement.** Vantrela Biotech is considering entering India with Dermacalm, a biologic for moderate-to-severe psoriasis. The board wants a go/no-go recommendation within a quarter.

**Business objective.** Decide whether to launch in India, and if so, what patient share and revenue to plan for by year 3.

**Data provided**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Psoriasis patients in India | 25,000,000 |
| Moderate-to-severe share | 20% |
| Diagnosed share (of moderate-to-severe) | 40% |
| Treated with systemic/biologic therapy | 15% |
| Eligible for a biologic | 60% of treated |
| Current biologic share: Comp A / Comp B / Others | 45% / 30% / 25% |
| Planned net price | ₹1,20,000 per patient per year |
| COGS as % of net price | 30% |
| Projected share by year 3 | 12% of eligible patients |
| Launch investment (one-time) | ₹150 Cr |
| Launch horizon | 3 years |

**Questions**
1. Compute the eligible patient pool and the SAM value.
2. Compute year-3 revenue at 12% share.
3. Compute contribution per patient and the break-even patient count.
4. Break-even as a % of the eligible pool — is that plausible given the competitive set?
5. What missing information matters most?
6. Recommendation task: go/no-go with two conditions.

**Calculations required:** percentage chain; unit economics; break-even; share reasonableness.

**Risks and assumptions to state:** diagnosis rate and treatment rate are the two most sensitive assumptions; price net of GTN; competitive response; supply/cold-chain feasibility; ramp timing (share by year 3 assumes 3 years of selling).

## Case 3 — Product adoption decline (fictional: NeuroY)

**Problem statement.** NeuroY prescriptions have fallen for three consecutive quarters despite stable sales-force activity. Leadership wants to know why — and what to do.

**Business objective.** Identify the driver of decline and recommend a corrective plan before the next quarter closes.

**Data provided**

| Quarter | TRx | New starts | Refills | Competitor share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Q1 | 120,000 | 42,000 | 78,000 | 0% (pre-launch) |
| Q2 | 118,000 | 40,000 | 78,000 | 6% |
| Q3 | 112,000 | 34,000 | 78,000 | 11% |
| Q4 | 104,000 | 26,000 | 78,000 | 15% |

Additional facts: aided awareness fell 64% → 58% over the year; a prior-authorisation requirement was added for 40% of covered lives in Q3; rep calls per physician were unchanged.

**Questions**
1. Compute the QoQ and total % declines in TRx.
2. Split the decline into new starts and refills — which explains it?
3. What is the most likely driver, and what is the alternative hypothesis?
4. Compute the "new-start run rate" if Q4's level continues for four quarters (with refills flat) and compare with Q1's run rate.
5. What would you check first, given limited time?
6. Recommend two interventions and how you would measure them.

**Calculations required:** % changes, decomposition, run-rate extrapolation.

# 9.12 Solutions

> Solutions are worked the way you should present them: units, chain, finding, implication.

## Case 1 solutions

**1 — Eligible pool 180,000; SAM ₹2,160 Cr.**
25,000,000 × 0.20 = 5,000,000 moderate-to-severe → × 0.40 = 2,000,000 diagnosed → × 0.15 = 300,000 treated → × 0.60 = **180,000 eligible**. SAM = 180,000 × ₹1,20,000 = **₹2,160 Cr**.

**2 — ₹259.2 Cr.** 12% × 180,000 = 21,600 patients × ₹1,20,000.

**3 — Contribution ₹84,000/patient; break-even 17,857 patients.** 1,20,000 × 0.70 = 84,000; ₹150 Cr ÷ ₹84,000 = **17,857 patients**.

**4 — Plausible.** Break-even is 17,857/180,000 = **9.9%** of the eligible pool, versus a target of 12%. But the competitive set (A 45%, B 30%) means you must take share from incumbents, not just ride category growth. Assessment: feasible but not comfortable; the margin of safety is thin (12% vs 9.9%).

**5 — Missing information (name the three that matter):** (i) real access conditions — does a prior authorisation exist, and what share of covered lives? (ii) persistence at 12 months; (iii) competitor response (price, contracts, new data). Also useful: physician-level adoption data, supply/cold-chain constraints, and the payer mix (out-of-pocket vs insured).

**6 — Recommendation.** Proceed with a **staged entry**: launch in 2–3 metro clusters with a small specialist sales force, contingent on (a) achieving ≥9.9% share-equivalent penetration of the eligible pool in the pilot, and (b) securing reimbursement for ≥50% of commercial lives. Otherwise delay and renegotiate pricing/access. **Risks:** diagnosis rate (40%) is the most sensitive assumption — a 10 pp error moves the SAM by ₹540 Cr (25%). **Next step:** commission a payer-access assessment and validate the diagnosis rate with claims data.

## Case 2 solutions

**1 — ₹121.5 Cr / ₹283.5 Cr / ₹445.5 Cr.**
- Y1: 900,000 × 3% = 27,000 × ₹45,000 = **₹121.5 Cr**
- Y2: 63,000 × ₹45,000 = **₹283.5 Cr**
- Y3: 99,000 × ₹45,000 = **₹445.5 Cr**

**2 — Cumulative contribution ₹552.8 Cr.** Total revenue = 850.5 Cr × 0.65.

**3 — Break-even revenue ₹115.4 Cr; break-even occurs in Year 1** (Y1 revenue alone is ₹121.5 Cr). ₹75 Cr ÷ 0.65 = ₹115.4 Cr.

**4 — Downturn scenario: adoption 1.5% / 5% / 9% → ₹60.75 Cr / ₹202.5 Cr / ₹364.5 Cr; break-even in Year 2.**
- Y1 13,500 × 45,000 = ₹60.75 Cr (below the ₹115.4 Cr threshold)
- Y2 45,000 × 45,000 = ₹202.5 Cr; cumulative = ₹263.25 Cr → break-even crossed ~27% into Y2 ((115.4 − 60.75)/202.5 = 27%).
- Insight: the plan absorbs a 50% Y1 shortfall and still breaks even within year 2 — the launch is robust on these assumptions, *provided* COGS stays at 35%.

**5 — Persistence 70% at 12 months reduces year-1 revenue by up to ~30%** on a patient-year price model if you naively assume 100% persistence; applying 70% gives roughly ₹85.05 Cr instead of ₹121.5 Cr in Y1 (0.70 × 121.5). Direction: lower; magnitude: proportional to the persistence shortfall. Also note new starts would continue, so the effect is on the *stock* of treated patients, not on demand.

**6 — Dashboard metrics:** (i) new-patient starts per month versus plan (leading indicator of demand), and (ii) access metrics — % of covered lives with unrestricted coverage and abandonment rate at the pharmacy (leaking demand). Adding persistence would be a close third.

## Case 3 solutions

**1 — Declines: Q2 −1.7%, Q3 −5.1%, Q4 −7.1%; year total −13.3%.**
- 118,000/120,000 − 1 = −1.67%
- 112,000/118,000 − 1 = −5.08%
- 104,000/112,000 − 1 = −7.14%
- 104,000/120,000 − 1 = −13.33%
The decline is **accelerating**.

**2 — The decline is entirely new starts.** Refills are flat at 78,000 in every quarter; new starts fell 42,000 → 26,000 (−38.1%). The installed patient base is stable; the problem is **acquisition**, not retention.

**3 — Most likely driver: access friction (prior authorisation for 40% of covered lives added in Q3) combined with a new competitor (15% share by Q4).** Alternative hypotheses: (i) awareness decay (64% → 58%) — plausible but awareness rarely falls that fast without an external cause; (ii) trial-to-repeat conversion problems (contradicted by flat refills); (iii) sales-force productivity (contradicted by unchanged calls). Test: split new starts by payer type — if the decline is concentrated in PA-affected plans, access is the driver.

**4 — Run rates.** Q1 annualised: 42,000 × 4 = 168,000 new starts/year. Q4-based: 26,000 × 4 = 104,000/year — a **loss of 64,000 new starts per year** (−38.1%). With flat refills, the installed base will decay over time as patients discontinue, so the revenue impact lags the new-start impact and persists.

**5 — Check first:** new starts by payer/plan type (PA-affected vs not) — fastest way to confirm or kill the access hypothesis.

**6 — Interventions and measurement:**
- **Payer/access:** enroll in PA-support services (electronic PA, patient support hub, co-pay assistance) and negotiate coverage; measure: abandonment rate, time-to-fill, PA approval rate, new starts in affected plans.
- **Physician activation:** re-target physicians who prescribed in Q1 but not in Q4 with clinical data and peer-to-peer programs; measure: reactivation rate (physicians writing ≥1 Rx after 90 days dormant).
Both should be measured with a control group of territories where the intervention is not deployed (difference-in-differences), otherwise the competitor's entry will be credited to whatever you did.

## Case 4 solutions

**1 — Achievement:** North 106.7% (480/450), South 103.3% (620/600), East **80.8%** (210/260), West 102.6% (390/380). Overall = 1,700/1,690 = **100.6%**.

**2 — Revenue per rep:** North ₹12.0 Cr, South ₹13.8 Cr, East **₹8.4 Cr**, West ₹13.0 Cr. Revenue per target physician: North ₹0.40 Cr, South ₹0.41 Cr, East **₹0.26 Cr**, West ₹0.35 Cr.

**3 — East is weak on both coverage and productivity.** Its revenue per rep (₹8.4 Cr) and per physician (₹0.26 Cr) are both the lowest, so it is *not* a target-setting artifact: East's target per rep (260/25 = **₹10.4 Cr**) is the *lowest* of all four regions (North 11.25, South 13.33, West 12.67) — the target is lenient per rep and East still misses it.

**4 — Gap ₹50 Cr.** Closing it at East's current productivity (₹8.4 Cr per rep) needs ~6 extra reps (50/8.4 = 5.95). At the peer-average productivity (₹12.1 Cr), it needs ~4.1 reps. Better still, fix productivity: lifting East to ₹12.1 Cr/rep with the existing 25 reps would produce ₹302.5 Cr, i.e. **₹92.5 Cr more than current** — enough to beat target without adding coverage.

**5 — Data to request:** territory-level call coverage/reach (are the 800 target physicians actually seen?), competitor share-of-voice and formulary status in East, physician mix and potential, and rep tenure/vacancy history (new reps ramp slowly — vacancy is a classic cause of a regional miss).

**6 — Recommendation:** do **not** simply add reps or reset the target. Run a 90-day diagnostic on coverage and access in East (vacancy, call quality, formulary), shift 2–3 reps temporarily from West/North where achievement is above target and productivity is high, and re-target the top 2 deciles of East physicians. Re-set the East target only after the coverage diagnosis — if the miss persists with full coverage, then the target itself is the problem.

## Case 5 solutions

**1 — Weighted regular-prescribing rate = 18.4%.** (100 × 0.35 + 250 × 0.18 + 150 × 0.08)/500 = (35 + 45 + 12)/500 = 92/500.

**2 — Monthly patients by regular prescribers:** Academic 100 × 45 × 0.35 = **1,575**; Community 250 × 25 × 0.18 = **1,125**; Low-volume 150 × 10 × 0.08 = **120**. Total = **2,820 patients/month**.
- Insight: academic physicians are 20% of the sample but 55.9% of the treated volume — but they are also the hardest to influence.

**3 — Biggest absolute uplift from +5 pp trial→regular conversion:**
- Academic: 100 physicians × 5 pp = 5 physicians × 45 patients = **225 patients/month**
- Community: 250 × 5 pp = 12.5 × 25 = **312.5 patients/month**
- Low-volume: 150 × 5 pp = 7.5 × 10 = **75 patients/month**
Community is the largest absolute opportunity (312.5), and is typically more responsive than academic KOLs.

**4 — Trial as a % of aware:** Academic 60/85 = **70.6%**; Community 38/62 = **61.3%**; Low-volume 20/45 = **44.4%**. Pattern: the higher the awareness, the higher the conversion to trial — consistent with awareness being a precondition; low-volume physicians have both low awareness and low conversion, suggesting a *reach* problem more than a belief problem.

**5 — Caveats:** equal weighting would overstate academic behaviour (n = 100 of 500). Each percentage has its own base (aware percentages are of all physicians in the segment; trial percentages are of all in the segment too, while "trial as % of aware" changes the base — always state it). Also, the sample is a survey, so stated adoption may exceed actual behaviour.

**6 — Where the next 10 reps go:** the **community segment** — largest absolute opportunity, reasonable conversion, and easier reach. Split: 7 community, 2 low-volume (awareness-building via digital/multichannel rather than reps), 1 academic (KOL/advocacy). Justify with the 312.5 vs 225 comparison and note the lower cost per incremental patient in community.

## Case 6 solutions

**1 — CIs.** Overall: SE = √(0.52 × 0.48/400) = 2.50 pp → MOE = 4.90 pp → **47.1%–52.9%**.
- Academic (n = 120, p = 0.63): SE = √(0.63 × 0.37/120) = 4.41 pp → MOE = 8.64 pp → **54.4%–71.6%**
- Community (n = 280, p = 0.47): SE = √(0.47 × 0.53/280) = 2.98 pp → MOE = 5.85 pp → **41.2%–52.9%**

**2 — Difference is statistically significant.** Counts: 75.6 → 76 academic "likely" and 131.6 → 132 community. Pooled p = (76 + 132)/400 = 0.52. SE of the difference = √(0.52 × 0.48 × (1/120 + 1/280)) = 5.45 pp. z = (63 − 47)/5.45 = **2.94** → p ≈ 0.003, well below 0.05. The CIs only just overlap, consistent with significance at α = 0.05.

**3 — Intensity vs behaviour.** 52% intent vs a mean of ~4.1 actual patient starts per month. Intent is a useful *relative* signal (academic vs community), but the behavioural measure should anchor the forecast. Convert behaviour to a volume: 400 physicians × 4.1 = 1,640 patient-starts/month — then note that the survey sample must be scaled to the real physician population before use.

**4 — A 38% PA barrier means revenue arrives late and leaks.** Expect: lower conversion of intent, higher abandonment at the pharmacy, and a longer time-to-first-fill. Model net revenue with an access factor (e.g., multiply the naive forecast by the share of covered lives with feasible access) rather than assuming full conversion.

**5 — Academic caveat:** n = 120 gives ±8.6 pp — the segment estimate is "between 54% and 72%", which is too wide to plan against precisely; also academics are over-sampled relative to the population, so the overall 52% may itself be biased upward unless weighted.

**6 — Recommendation:** run **access-first**. The behavioural evidence (4.1 starts/pharmacist month) and the 38% PA barrier imply that the constraint is access and activation, not belief. Launch promotion → enrol in payer access programs and co-pay support in parallel, and track new starts by plan type. Defer heavy DTC-style spending until access is resolved.

## Case 7 solutions

**1 — Market and brand revenue.**
- 2024 market = ₹4,000/1.12 = **₹3,571.4 Cr**
- A: 38% × 3,571.4 = **₹1,357.1 Cr** → 33% × 4,000 = **₹1,320 Cr**
- B: 22% × 3,571.4 = **₹785.7 Cr** → 27% × 4,000 = **₹1,080 Cr**
- C: 16% × 3,571.4 = **₹571.4 Cr** → 19% × 4,000 = **₹760 Cr**
- D: 12% × 3,571.4 = **₹428.6 Cr** → 10% × 4,000 = **₹400 Cr**
- Others: 12% × 3,571.4 = **₹428.6 Cr** → 11% × 4,000 = **₹440 Cr**

**2 — Revenue changes:** B **+37.5%** (1,080/785.7 − 1), C **+33.0%** (760/571.4 − 1), Others **+2.7%**, A **−2.7%** (1,320/1,357.1 − 1), D **−6.7%** (400/428.6 − 1). Ranking: B > C > Others > A > D.

**3 — A's price cut failed to defend revenue.** A's revenue *fell* 2.7% while the market grew 12%, meaning A lost share *and* absolute revenue: the 10% price cut did not buy enough volume to compensate (a 10% price cut requires >11.1% volume growth to hold revenue flat; A likely gained little volume because access, not price, was the constraint). Classic case of discounting into a demand problem.

**4 — Lost share went mostly to B and C.** A lost 5 pp; B gained 5 pp and C gained 3 pp (Others −1, D −2, net consistent). In revenue terms relative to expected: had A held 38% of the 2025 market it would have earned ₹1,520 Cr — the actual ₹1,320 Cr implies **₹200 Cr of foregone revenue**, most plausibly captured by B (which gained ~₹294 Cr of revenue overall, much of it from category growth plus share) and C (~₹189 Cr).

**5 — Recommended actions for A:** (i) stop price-led competition and rebuild the **access/coverage** position (formulary contracts, PA support) — the evidence says the constraint is access, not price; (ii) sharpen differentiation versus C's new formulation with data/real-world evidence and KOL programs, and re-target the deciles where switching is still fluid.

**6 — Before any further price cuts:** model the **price elasticity** properly (what volume uplift actually occurred after the cut, with a control), understand GTN/rebate implications (a list-price cut often hits net price disproportionately), check payer contract structure (step therapy thresholds), and confirm whether the competitor can respond even lower — a price war with a larger, better-covered competitor is unwinnable.

## 9.13 Follow-up drills (say the answer out loud in ≤60 seconds)

1. "Your SAM assumes a 40% diagnosis rate. What if it's 25%?" *(SAM falls to 25,000,000 × 0.20 × 0.25 × 0.15 × 0.60 = 112,500 patients = ₹1,350 Cr, and break-even rises to 17,857/112,500 = 15.9% of the eligible pool — materially harder than the planned 12% share.)*
2. "Why would you stage the launch instead of going national?" *(Limits capital at risk, generates real-world access data, surfaces objections early; the trade-off is slower share accumulation and competitor first-mover advantage.)*
3. "What single number would you want before signing off?" *(For Case 1: % of covered lives with unrestricted access. For Case 6: the access-adjusted conversion rate.)*
4. "How would you validate a survey-based adoption number?" *(Compare with prescription/claims data, apply an intent-to-behaviour discount, and test a pilot territory.)*
5. "You have one week and one analyst. What do you do?" *(Pick the hypothesis with the largest decision impact — usually access — design the smallest test that can confirm or refute it, and report the range, not a single number.)*
